Are there natural laws governing food?

Instinct and Natural Laws

Our culture and philosophy have become so detached from nature that we sometimes no longer recognize natural laws. We sometimes hear their existence denied, in the name of a certain view of anthropology: “Man is a social animal,” “everything depends on culture,” “it’s all a matter of conditioning”… Cultural relativism is a convenient excuse: since everything is supposedly relative to the conditioning experienced by the individual, the individual would adapt to anything. It would therefore be absurd to speak of natural laws of human behavior, especially when it comes to food.

This language has been used by many pseudo-philosophers of gastronomy, during conferences or television programs. Humans are certainly endowed with an intelligence that allows them to modify nature through all sorts of artifice; But since artifice is part of human nature, this reasoning suggests there is no longer any need to distinguish between natural law and paradoxical behavior.

But let’s leave the controversies aside and try to see what the situation is exactly.

Defining Natural Law

It is possible to define a whole series of criteria that allow us to draw the line between what falls under natural law and what falls under cultural artifice. We can consider that we are dealing with a natural law when a phenomenon obeys the following criteria:

Regularity:
The phenomenon must be observed regularly when certain conditions are met; for example, an unpleasant sensation appears regularly when one consumes raw food (unprocessed, uncooked, and unmixed) beyond a certain point.
Universality:
The same phenomenon must be observed in all individuals; The variation in taste perception follows the same regular patterns in a European, an Asian, an African, an Indian, a vegetarian, etc., regardless of their eating habits.
Independence from the observer:
The phenomenon must not depend on the deliberate will of the observer; for example, the variation in flavor cannot be suppressed by an act of concentration.
Reaction to interference:
An interference with the spontaneous development of the phenomenon must result in a change in its direct or indirect manifestations; in our example, consuming food beyond the point at which a change in flavor is detected is regularly followed by digestive or other disturbances.

When these conditions are met, the observed phenomenon can be considered to obey a natural law; in our example, it can be defined as an alliesthetic mechanism obeying a law of negation when food intake exceeds digestive capacity.

Every natural law manifests itself only under given conditions, so other “adjacent” natural laws can be associated with it—that is, other regularities that appear as a function of changes in these conditions. For example, the amount of food that can be consumed before alliesthetic response becomes negative varies from one individual to another. If it is observed that these variations correspond to certain needs of the body—if the number of calories consumed increases with energy expenditure, for example—then the fact that alliesthetic mechanisms regulate nutritional intake can be considered a natural law.

This reasoning can be extended if, in the long term, it is observed that alliesthetic mechanisms lead to nutritional balance—verifiable either by calculating nutritional intake based on food components, by blood tests to identify deficiency indicators, or by normalizing indirect symptoms such as obesity or inflammatory tendencies. We can then consider that consuming food in its unmodified form, allowing alliesthetic mechanisms to function unimpeded, constitutes a natural law of nutrition.

Any natural law concerning living beings can be related to the notion of genetic programming: if the alliesthetic phenomenon does not appear in certain individuals carrying a mutation or a brain lesion, it can be considered genetically determined. The fact that it does not manifest, or that it does not fulfill its regulatory function when food is processed, can then be interpreted as a genetic maladaptation to foods modified by culinary artifice. The natural law will then be stated as the correct expression of the organism’s genetic potential, the necessary condition of which is that the flavors of food conform to their natural characteristics.

This reasoning, used here with the example of gustatory alliesthesia, actually applies to all observable biological or psychological phenomena: whenever certain situations promote vital functions, while others produce disorders, suffering, or trauma, the boundary between these two categories allows us to define a natural law. It goes without saying that many situations considered culturally normal can be harmful: the concept of natural law is therefore essential for defining the limits of culture and explaining the suffering that is too often associated with it.

Application to Nutrition

First, it is quite clear that the human body needs food to survive. In extreme cases, it can survive for a certain period on intravenous fluids, which are supposed to provide the essential nutrients and water without which all life is impossible. One can also feed an individual via a gastric tube, or give them pills concentrating all the necessary nutritional components, but these methods are only applicable for relatively short periods. Astronauts themselves dream of fresh produce after a stay in orbit, and the nutritionists who manage their diet ensure a minimum intake of fruits and vegetables, as expensive as transporting a kilogram of goods by space shuttle.

More trivially, if an individual is deprived of all food, they deteriorate—except for a few yogis or saints who claim to nourish themselves on the air or the sun’s rays, without anyone ever being able to prove that the complete fast they advocate isn’t interspersed with clandestine food intake. The fact remains that ordinary people like us need food to survive. This alone demonstrates that certain natural laws exist, the first of which is that the body must be nourished to perform its functions.

Can we then take it a step further and say that the body must be nourished correctly in order to perform its functions correctly? It seems obvious today, but there was a time when it was fashionable to grant humans total freedom: what they ate was thought to have no influence on their health, the essential thing being the pleasure they derived from it, the social connection, the conviviality, the symbols represented by the food rather than its biochemical content.

Molecules from Cooking: Empirical Proof

A comedian from Lausanne, after giving some major lectures on the subject, summed up this opposition in a striking title: “Me, Mr. Burger, I don’t eat molecules!” At the time, it was not at all common to think that food could contain harmful molecules from cooking. Yet today, this is widely proven.

As early as 1917, the American chemist Maillard, searching for the substances responsible for the characteristic flavor of roasted potatoes, observed that all sorts of molecules are formed under the effect of heat. The “Maillard reaction” molecules, however, remained largely unknown, and it was only after World War II that research on them slowly began to increase. Then, at the end of the century, research on diabetes led to the definition of AGEs (Advanced Glycation Endproducts), molecules resulting from chemical reactions between proteins and glucose, which form in the blood when blood sugar levels are too high.

It took another decade before it was realized that these AGEs are mostly formed during cooking-related chemical reactions, that they pass into the bloodstream, and that they can cause all sorts of damage. The case of acrylamide, discovered in 2002, marked a new stage: spectacular quantities of this molecule, whose toxicity was already known, were found in potato chips, French fries, and other foods subjected to intensive heat treatment. The WHO had long limited its use in water disinfection to 1 µg per liter, while the levels found in the foods examined exceeded this limit a thousandfold.

Several research teams continue to track molecules produced by cooking chemical reactions. Recent publications have defined ALEs (Advanced Lipid Endproducts), fats altered by the heating of food, whose toxicity is beyond doubt.

The mere fact that molecules produced by cooking processes are toxic demonstrates that there are natural laws of nutrition, that is, limits beyond which food can be harmful to health. This stems directly from the definition of a natural law explained above: it describes a condition necessary for maintaining health, or sufficient to induce bodily dysfunction.

The resulting natural law is that it is not natural to cook food, especially at high temperatures—or rather, that foods transformed by the application of high temperatures do not correspond to the genetic makeup of the human body.

Some have long argued that cooking at moderate temperatures is harmless, generating far fewer chemical reactions. Unfortunately, more recent studies demonstrate that AGEs (Advanced Glycation End Products) are already synthesized in large quantities at around 100°C, which calls into question boiling, pressure cooking, and even steaming.

Common sense suggests, based on the production of AGEs in the blood of diabetics—where they were first discovered—that temperatures of 37°C are sufficient to trigger glycation reactions (the binding of glucose to other molecules), the toxicity of which explains the various forms of degeneration resulting from excessive blood sugar levels. Therefore, it is not only temperature that determines AGE production, but also glucose concentration—hence the importance of sugar and starches, glucose chains frequently used in cooking.

Ultimately, if cooking generates harmful molecules and if, on the other hand, a raw food diet is sufficient to ensure good health, we can state the following natural law: the human body needs a raw food diet to function properly.

Beyond Molecules: The Role of Flavors

However, it’s not just the molecular nature of food that matters, but also its organoleptic characteristics: when a beneficial product tastes bad, we eat less of it; when a harmful product is too appealing, we tend to consume it in excess. It’s therefore logical to ask what happens when we prepare food in such a way as to make it as palatable as possible—and conversely, what the effects would be of a diet that avoids any preparation to enhance natural flavors.

Only experience can answer these questions: simply compare the nutritional balance achieved with a prepared diet to that achieved with an unprepared diet, as advocated by instinctonutrition. The first method, tested universally on humans as well as in laboratories on a few cages of guinea pigs, results in a significant percentage of overweight or obesity. The second, on the contrary, ensures a regular height and weight balance, provided one listens to their body.

A more precise criterion is that of inflammation: with a raw, unprocessed diet, the inflammatory tendency is spontaneously maintained within limits such that the classic signs of inflammation—edema, erythema, pain—do not appear in the event of injury. This phenomenon also allows us to define a whole series of rules that must be respected, most of which consist of getting rid of bad habits acquired in the previous dietary context.

This allows us to state another natural law, complementary to the previous ones: the human body needs a raw and unprocessed diet to spontaneously ensure proper nutritional balance.

Conclusion

The definition of the natural laws of nutrition is therefore based essentially on empirical observation, according to the criteria of regularity, universality, independence from the observer, and response to interference outlined above. By recognizing certain limits, the transgression of which leads to functional disorders, we can define the nutritional conditions that guarantee the proper functioning of the body. These limits are determined more by genetics than by cultural conditioning, as demonstrated by their universality and their independence from previous dietary patterns.

Ultimately, instinctotherapy simply defines these natural laws and teaches their application, particularly the various causes of involuntary transgression that could compromise its beneficial effects.
