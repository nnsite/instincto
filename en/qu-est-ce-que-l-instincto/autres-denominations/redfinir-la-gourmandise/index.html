Redefining gluttony

Brillat-Savarin, a famous cantor of the art of gastronomy, noted two centuries ago that the notion of gluttony was not clearly defined in dictionaries. It was confused with gluttony, while true gluttony was for him an art of frugality, aimed at pleasure through perfection and intensity of sensations, not quantity. Gluttony as he conceived it represented a guarantee of health, leading to consuming the most appropriate foods, while avoiding the nuisance of overeating. His famous treatise Physiology of taste or Meditations on transcendent gastronomy dates from 1825. Here are some extracts:

I scanned the dictionaries using the word gluttony, and I was not satisfied with what I found there … It is only a perpetual confusion of gluttony proper with gluttony and voracity. .. Gluttony is the enemy of excess … (it) also includes delicacies, which is none other than the same preference applied to light, delicate dishes, of little volume … result and proof of the healthy and perfect state of the organs intended for nutrition … Morally, it is an implicit resignation to the orders of the Creator, who, having ordered us to eat in order to live, invites us to do so by appetite, sustains us with flavor, and rewards us with pleasure.

It is clear that gluttony is a natural human faculty. We will even see how avant-garde Brillat-Savarin’s claims are. However, we must ask ourselves if this faculty can be expressed correctly in the context of the traditional diet, and what it becomes - or rather: what it could be - under the primitive feeding conditions.

Gluttony appeals to the pleasure of the senses. However, the senses do not necessarily work in the same way with natural foods or with processed foods. The laws of evolution, for example, had to organize the sense of taste in such a way that it stimulates the consumption of foods useful to organisms, and avoids the absorption of harmful foods. This for obvious reasons of natural selection: an animal strain which would have displayed a predilection for harmful foods would have simply sidelined, it would have been eliminated by the ruthless competition laws.

Since too much useful food becomes harmful, nature even had to endow the sense of taste with a system of attraction – repulsion capable of transforming sensation from positive to negative. Overly greedy animals would have put themselves in a state of inferiority, as a result of digestive overload or obesity. Brillat-Savarin attributes to the Creator what Darwin would deduce from the laws of Evolution.

However, this system of inversion of taste perceptions is also observed in humans: this is what scientists have called taste alliesthesia. Experience shows that the pleasure of the palate can go from positive to negative during consumption. But this mechanism doesn’t - or doesn’t work as well - with just any food. For example, it works perfectly with strawberries, if possible wild strawberries, eaten as they are: after a certain quantity, the first wonderfully fragrant flavor turns unpleasant, to the point of irritating the tongue. While the same strawberries prepared with powdered sugar and whipped cream are still pleasing to the palate, even if consumed in disproportionate amounts, until the stomach cramps. A feeling of fullness arises to avoid a notorious excess, but the flavor itself remains fascinating.

The same rule holds true with most foods: the alliesthesic shift manifests itself with raw food, it is only a shadow of itself when it is transformed by any culinary artifice. Quite simply because culinary tricks have been invented over time to make foods that are not necessarily pleasant to the palate.

What can we deduce from this in terms of gluttony?

It is first possible to give it a clear and distinct definition: gluttony consists in seeking the pleasure of the palate with foods that are of no use to the body.

From this immediately follows an essential theorem: gluttony thus defined is in principle not possible with foods left in their natural form, since they are, for reasons of evolution of species, pleasing to the palate only when they are useful to the body. And the converse: it is possible with processed foods, because the art of cooking makes them tasty even when they are unnecessary or even harmful to the body.

The only problem with unprocessed foods is that you may tend, for psychological reasons, to consume excessive amounts.

Result: the level of flavor decreases day by day, there is no longer any question of gluttony (given the disappearance of pleasure), and we must then speak of gluttony, gluttony, bulimia, etc; Brillat-Savarin was therefore right when he made the equation between gluttony and frugality.

In other words, gluttony does not exist in nature. It appears as a direct consequence of human artifice, capable of transforming food to make it cross the barrier put in place by millions of years of evolution and supposed to allow only useful food to pass. That is to say, the human intelligence is able to thwart natural laws in order to experience pleasures at the expense of its physical and even psychological integrity. The failure of alliesthesia, blurring the first experiences of pleasure, can have serious consequences on psychic structuring, a fact whose consequences psychoanalysis has not yet measured.

We find on the one hand the idealistic position of Brillat-Savarin (his treatise aimed at “transcendent gastronomy”), and the religious point of view which made gluttony a sin, that is to say a violation of natural laws. . To sin actually comes from “rata”, a Hebrew word meaning “to miss the mark”. Natural gluttony, as it is expressed with unprocessed foods, “deserves only praise and encouragement” (dixit BS), while in the culinary context, it leads to the absorption of foods that one should not. to absorb, this at the expense of both the consumer and the food sources offered to us by the planet: it then twice misses its original target, which was aimed at maintaining health and preserving the environment.

It is clear that Brillat-Savarin, ignoring the notion of evolution and a great defender of gastronomy, did not know or wanted to make this distinction … He concluded his panegyric thus: “In whatever relation one considers gluttony , it only deserves praise and encouragement ”. We can agree with it, on condition of renouncing the artifices which alter the natural flavors: with the foods consumed in their original form, the pleasure such as nature put it in place, “at the orders of the Creator”, indeed guarantees optimum balance of vital functions.

In practice: gluttony shows the tip of the ear with fruits or other selected products, when they have easier flavors than the primitive products. The tendency to overload is obvious, verified by many criteria (dyspesia, increased inflammatory tendency, pain, etc.). Learning is necessary to correctly set the threshold for alliesthesia negativation, as well as particularly attentive listening to repletion signals.
