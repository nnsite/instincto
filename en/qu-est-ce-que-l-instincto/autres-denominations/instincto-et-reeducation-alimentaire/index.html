Instinct and Dietary Re-education

Who doesn’t complain about a certain perversion of the senses that supposedly leads humans (both men and women) to devour all sorts of harmful foods, while animals know perfectly well how to refuse what harms them?

It’s true that a fox won’t eat the belladonna it encounters, which is toxic to it because it lacks the necessary enzymes to break down atropine. The deer, on the other hand, relishes it and is not poisoned at all, having, like all herbivores, the appropriate enzymes. The poison breaks down in its digestive tract like any other food component, and its instinct would be wrong to deprive it of this highly nutritious berry.

Why aren’t humans protected against this poisonous plant, when it is dangerous to the body, even fatal from just a few berries? In reality, it is also protected: no enticing aroma compels it to taste this elegant nightshade; if it does, it is solely out of curiosity or inadvertently. A blueberry picker might mistake them for blueberries and make a dessert with disastrous consequences. Mixed with blueberries, sugar, and whipped cream, the sense of taste is powerless to protect the consumer. Belladonna tasted on its own, without mixing or any other alteration, has an unappealing flavor, and only a conscious effort will compel one to ingest it.

This example clearly illustrates the limits of natural protection: the absence of an enticing scent is the first barrier; indeed, a deliberate act is required to put it in the mouth; curiosity is thus capable of overcoming instinct; flavor constitutes a second protection, provided, here too, that one does not force oneself to swallow a tasteless product, nor sweeten it with any kind of preparation.

However, the same rule applies to all foods and all natural poisons: as Jean-Jacques Rousseau already noted, no toxic plant is appealing to the senses unless it is presented in its unaltered state. But an odorless and tasteless plant is not necessarily non-toxic. This is the first and most basic form of re-education that instinct allows: never put food in your mouth that is not appealing to the smell, and never swallow anything that is not stimulating to the taste.

Seemingly very simple, this rule requires a difficult shift away from the habits instilled by our upbringing: one spoonful for Mom, one spoonful for Dad, finish your plate, eat your soup to grow up, there are poor people who would be happy to eat that, etc. From the earliest age, education strives to instill in children the denial of their own instincts and force them to swallow what is imposed upon them – a pattern that later manifests in their relationships, social interactions, and political life.

The remedy lies in listening to the body. This means not only never running a red light, but only proceeding when the light is green, that is, consuming only foods that are appealing in every respect in their raw form. The main barriers are smell, taste, texture, and satiety. The corollary is that one must have acquired, almost as a reflex, the understanding that processed foods can be appealing (or not repulsive) even though they are harmful. French fries, for example, are very attractive, despite being contaminated by toxic (trans) fatty acids and other highly harmful substances such as acrylamide or Maillard reaction products. The same goes for an omelet with death cap mushrooms, deadly despite its delicious egg and mushroom flavor.

However, re-education goes much further: it involves learning to recognize the nuances and infinite variations in natural flavors that reflect the body’s needs. A child who hasn’t learned language during the so-called sensitive period can never acquire normal language. Similarly, someone who hasn’t learned early on to distinguish the countless facets of natural flavors will have great difficulty regaining normal sensitivity and the ability to discriminate. In this respect, instinct is the best possible form of re-education. It teaches us not only to become aware of the messages of the senses, but it also ensures a kind of correction of sensations, akin to animal training: each transgression of a sensory signal is accompanied by feelings of discomfort, whereas attentive listening allows us to achieve maximum well-being.

The universal principle of the carrot and the stick (of reward and punishment) is thus at work to gradually rebuild the natural functioning of the eating instinct. This form of harmony then extends to the rest of life: nothing is more precious than being able to listen to one’s deepest intuitions; this is how one can avoid serious errors in daily decisions and choices.

A third aspect is added to the above: the inner stance towards “temptation.” Giving up familiar foods, sweets, sauces, and other seductive culinary delights proves extremely difficult at first.

The bottomless pit phenomenon, intrinsically linked to alliesthetic mechanisms in the culinary context, constitutes a vicious cycle on several levels: first, the simple physiological phenomenon, which produces a decrease in satisfaction with natural foods as soon as an overload (even a slight one) is induced by a processed food that bypasses the alliesthetic barrier. Re-education here consists of becoming aware that culinary pleasures ultimately represent a decrease in natural pleasure, and that these natural pleasures, once the body has detoxified, are far superior to artificial pleasures.

Added to this is the problem of familial and social conditioning: sweets and good meals are associated with parental love and conviviality from the earliest childhood. Just as alcohol is associated with celebration, friendship, and good deals, or as cigarettes were once associated with the image of virility, these associations are actually perverse, since the underlying artifice is harmful to health and ultimately degrades true joy of living. Rediscovering the natural connections between the pleasure of taste, deep satisfaction, well-being, health, and joy of living is undoubtedly the most valuable re-education offered by the practice of natural eating.

Over time, an increasingly clear relationship develops between the body’s proprioception and the foods likely to support it. This correspondence, however, is never absolute and must always be filtered through the senses of smell and taste. It does, however, allow us to guide our testing and source suitable foods.
