Nutritional balance

We can distinguish two forms of nutritional balance: the balance of food intake, defined by dietetics — this involves absorbing the quantities of calories, carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, minerals and trace elements recommended by nutritional science — and the functional balance of the organism, which involves intake corresponding to individual needs in real time.

The body can only function properly if it receives adequate amounts of the various nutrients it needs. Intakes must fall between a minimum and a maximum threshold, otherwise there is a risk of deficiency or overload, with their respective harms: the blocking of certain functions, excessive weight gain, or damage to organs.

The first form of balance, that of dietetics, is defined from average needs calculated over a given population and over a long period of time. It runs up against a fundamental paradox: *nutritional needs vary unpredictably from one individual to another and from one moment to the next, so that a diet that is “balanced” in the dietetic sense is necessarily physiologically unbalancing. A virus, physical exertion, a period of rest, recovery, or detoxification is enough for foods that are usually beneficial to become harmful from one moment to the next, or for the desirable quantities to be reduced.

This paradox has serious consequences, because it is precisely when the body is in difficulty — under the influence of a virus, bacteria, or a state of stress — that needs deviate from the average, and that it becomes essential to respond to them as accurately as possible.

The rule generally taught is to eat a little of everything and nothing in excess. This amounts to a double begging of the question, since it is precisely a matter of knowing, for each food, what quantities are indispensable and where the thresholds of excess lie. Nothing guarantees that a quantity which seems reasonable is not already excessive: an alcoholic, for example, will consider that his liter of wine a day is nothing excessive.

The concept of a varied diet rests on the idea that traditional food would contain all the foods useful to health and none that are harmful. Yet advances in research show that tradition is no guarantee. Fried foods, common to all traditional cuisines, are a well-known cause of cardiovascular disease. Grilled foods, considered in the mid-twentieth century to be a panacea thanks to the absence of heated oils, have proven to be highly carcinogenic. Milk has been promoted as an indispensable source of calcium, whereas the necessary amounts are amply found in vegetables and fruit, and the calcium content of cow’s milk — three times higher than that of human breast milk — can pose problems for the infant’s future health. All these foods remain tempting even though they are useless or harmful.

In this respect, dietetics comes up against an insurmountable difficulty: it is not possible to know from the outside what is happening inside an organism. In the case of illness, for example, one must wait for a diagnosis before drawing conclusions; dietetics cannot therefore adjust food intake to the body’s needs in real time — it is forced to wait for symptoms to appear before establishing a prescription, one that is itself open to question, since it is based on average values that erase individual singularities.

Added to this is a second difficulty: the complexity of known nutritional needs. Nutritional science has identified a large number of nutrients essential to the body’s functioning, so that correctly adjusting intake for each component is practically unworkable: this requires inextricable calculations and also runs up against universal gluttony.

A third difficulty: the complexity of physiological mechanisms, known and unknown, such that the inferences one can draw about the body’s needs from current knowledge remain highly questionable. If an individual is given the nutrients deemed necessary in isolated form — so many grams of protein, so many milligrams of calcium, so many units of vitamin C — in powder form, with an amount of pure water equivalent to that found in natural foods, he will not be long in experiencing all sorts of difficulties. The dietary model currently in force is thus far removed from reality, and the reasoning that can be drawn from it is unreliable. For it to be reliable, one would need to know everything about biological mechanisms, which is not, and probably never will be, the case.

Foods are in fact extremely complex biological realities, and the approach taken by nutritional science remains very crude. A walnut, for example, cannot be reduced to the substances chemical analysis alone can detect in it, since many subtle substances slip through the net, and molecules can take different forms. Fats illustrate this well: once lumped together as a single category, advances in knowledge have led to distinguishing saturated from unsaturated fatty acids, then polyunsaturated ones, then the omega series, then cis from trans fatty acids — and many characteristics essential to health no doubt remain to be discovered.

There is, however, a way of taking the body’s real needs into account, an agency in contact with the internal environment and able to take them into account in real time: the dietary instinct. This situation stems directly from the loss of the natural guidance once exercised by the sense organs: smell and taste have always had as their essential function the detection and dosing of useful foods. The smell of rancidity, for example, protects against carcinogenic substances that appear through oxidation. Under natural conditions, no animal eats without first smelling the food, and it stops once taste and other internal signals indicate that its needs are met.

Such regulation is made possible by the senses’ capacity for modulation: a food changes in smell and taste according to the body’s needs or its state of overload. This function, named since the 1970s “olfactory and gustatory alliesthesia,” has been the subject of numerous publications, without ever having been integrated by nutritional science or by dietetics. Alliesthesic variations in smell, taste, disgust, stomach sensations, and so on were developed over millions of years of evolution and are capable of handling the complexity of living things, simply because they have always been part of it. The rudiments of this are already found in bacteria in the form of chemotaxis, a function that could only progress and grow more complex along with biological mechanisms over time. Experience indeed shows, in cases of illness or stress for example, that olfactory and taste attractions vary in a way that guarantees the correct intake of useful substances — this regulation obviously not working with foods whose flavors have been altered.

This is no doubt one of the most notable contributions of instinctotherapy: to base food equilibration on the senses of smell and taste, that is, to eat what is attractive to the senses, and to heed any sign of displeasure. Despite its universality in the natural world, the rule is unfortunately inapplicable to processed foods, for the simple reason that culinary preparation aims precisely to make foods more pleasant to the palate than they are in their raw form. A second rule must therefore be applied: to avoid any alteration of foods’ “organoleptic” characteristics — smell, taste, texture. Experience then shows that attraction to foods left in their raw form, provided they are close enough to primitive varieties, does indeed change according to the body’s needs and states of overload. With a certain period of rehabilitation, instinctotherapy restores these capacities, so that it becomes possible to supply the body with the substances it needs in quantities corresponding exactly to individual needs and tolerances.

At first glance these rules may seem restrictive, since they require giving up culinary artifice. But they actually allow access to levels of enjoyment well beyond gastronomic pleasures, and to an incomparable form of serenity: pleasure (once again) becomes the natural guide toward dietary balance and, with it, health. Calculations of food rations and the dietitian’s concerns give way to the spontaneity of pleasure, to trust in one’s own body and in the natural laws that govern the mysteries of life.

Nutritional balance ensured by this natural route is far more precise than that obtained through conventional means, and plays a role that is today little recognized in maintaining or restoring health. It is provided *from within, independent of diagnostic errors or errors of reasoning induced by insufficient knowledge of vital mechanisms, and it operates *in real time, adapted to *individual singularities. It therefore promotes the natural regulation of all kinds of disorders and pathologies even before symptoms have appeared, which gives it a genuine *therapeutic, *preventive, and curative function.
