The original food range

While we are still genetically adapted to the diets of our hominid ancestors, the question remains what exactly they ate. And also, knowing when to go back to define the diet that would best suit today’s man.

However, no one left us any reliable documents, it is hardly if we find an almost complete skeleton from time to time dating from before the fire. What then are the methods that would make it possible to get an idea of it?

There are all the same several, of which the overlaps can be significant.

  1. Compare the food range of the primates closest to us still living in the wild.
  2. Observe the food range as determined by our senses of smell and taste, supposed to attract us to natural foods that correspond to our digestive and metabolic characteristics.
  3. observe the effects on the digestive balance and on the health of the various natural foods that we can still find today.
  4. Trying to determine from the traces on the teeth of human skeletons what foods were eaten by our Hominian ancestors.
  5. Observe the diet of ethnic groups that have remained as close as possible to archaic eating habits

The first path is compromised by the fact that we are neither chimpanzees, nor gorillas, nor orangutans. The observation of the feeding behavior of these primates in nature cannot be transposed directly to humans. However, it can provide us with essential information on the origins of our nutritional structures. We share 99% of our genes with the chimpanzee, we can expect to share many characteristics in our digestive system and our metabolism as well.

A very recent study, conducted by a Japanese team who went to live in the field with these primates, found that their taste inclinations are very similar to those of humans. Their diet consists of 68% fruit, 28% foliage and green stems, and 5% animal proteins (insects, birds, antelopes, wild pigs, little monkeys). The protein intake provided by fruits is not negligible, it represents about 5% of their weight.

The chimpanzee is largely omnivorous, apart from the fruits, it readily eats leaves (two hundred species), buds, pieces of bark, resin, honey, termites, ants, sometimes other small mammals. . He is even interested in medicinal plants.

These data are to be compared with the food range determined by the human olfactory and taste attractions (way n ° 2). The practice of instinctotherapy, based on the sole criterion of pleasure, leads to appetites and comparable proportions: two thirds of fruit, a quarter of greens and vegetables, the rest in oilseeds, protein crops and animal proteins.

There is therefore a very significant overlap here: even though the learning of flavors took place in the culinary context for most individuals, the confrontation with uncooked foods spontaneously leads to rediscovering the proportions of the different classes of foods consumed by the descendants of our common distant ancestors, who have always lived in nature. The human line and that of chimpanzees have separated for 6 to 13 million years, depending on the source, there is between them the whole gap that separates culture from nature and, yet, the appetites are almost identical. It is in fact the culinary art which gives the false impression according to which the respective food ranges are different: it suffices to reduce the observations to a “preculinary” food context (elimination of cooking and preparations) to reveal a remarkable similarity.

It then remains to determine more precisely which species of fruits and plants correspond ideally to our digestive and metabolic data. It is not guaranteed a priori that the most attractive products for the senses will also be the most useful for the organism. There is also nothing to say from the outset that the most attractive fruits are the most important in the original food palette. To this is added another unknown: the products that we can dispose of are for the most part resulting from an artificial selection process tending to make them more pleasant to the palate than wild products: taking into account the simple taste attraction is not necessary. therefore not decisive. Only long-term experience, showing the effects on the functioning of the organism, can provide an answer to these questions.

This experience is not straightforward, for the reason that many foods vary from extreme to extreme, sometimes being very appealing and, in other situations, uninteresting if not repulsive. We must therefore take into account the level of attraction, and the frequency of these attractions, and that on different people in different situations. A fruit like durian, for example, is extremely fragrant and of a more appealing flavor than most other fruits; it is possible to consume it very regularly, most often with a high level of pleasure, even if it sometimes loses all interest or becomes frankly repellent (especially for wild varieties). Chimpanzees and other hominids show the same predilection for this fruit. Its effects on the body are positive: we can therefore say that it occupies an important place in the human food palette as in that of other primates. These findings go hand in hand with what we may know about the origin of primates, which are mainly found in equatorial and tropical regions, where this fruit itself originated.
